\begin{abstract}
We benchmark the FMM3D-accelerated dielectric boundary-integral solver used to assemble the
four-index integrals $V_{ijkl}$ of monolayer graphene. Three questions are addressed, each backed
by the measurements in \texttt{../data/}: (i) how the thin-slab interface geometry compares with a
3D-uniform point cloud; (ii) the speedup actually obtained from OpenMP threads and from batching
right-hand sides (\nd); and (iii) the mechanism by which the multi-RHS formulation accelerates the
solve. The headline results are: the slab geometry costs up to $7\times$ a uniform cloud at equal
problem size; thread scaling saturates near 32--64 cores ($\sim$18--20$\times$ at 96), while \nd{}
batching gives a flat $\sim$3.7$\times$, the two compounding to $\sim$73$\times$; and the full
block solve inherits the FMM's $\sim$4$\times$ per-RHS amortization, which we trace---from the
FMM3D Fortran and a direct phase breakdown---to per-interaction geometry and translation operators
being computed once and reused across all densities, \emph{not} to tree construction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup and methodology}\label{sec:setup}

All data are from the monolayer graphene model (cRPA data \texttt{k\_323201\_nb\_144\_c\_15}): a
dielectric slab of lateral size $L=90$, thickness $L_z=3.35$ (bohr), $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{in}}=3.5$,
discretized by panel quadrature on the box faces. The FMM kernel is Laplace \texttt{lfmm3d} from
FMM3D. Runs are on Flatiron \texttt{genoa} nodes (96 cores, \SI{1.5}{TB}); every thread
configuration ran on its \emph{own exclusive node} (Slurm array, one node per \texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS})
so the timings are free of core-sharing contention. Threads were pinned by setting
\emph{both} \texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS} and \texttt{OPENBLAS\_NUM\_THREADS}.

Throughput is reported as $\Theta = N\,\nd / t_{\mathrm{fmm}}$ in million source$\cdot$RHS
evaluations per second. The FMM cost is modeled as $t_{\mathrm{fmm}}(\nd)\approx a + b\,\nd$, with
$a$ the \nd-independent (shared) work and $b$ the per-density (marginal) work; the per-RHS
amortization ceiling is then $(a+b)/b$.

\section{Uniform versus non-uniform point distributions}\label{sec:geom}

To isolate the effect of geometry, we ran the identical FMM kernel ($\nd$, $N$, threads,
tolerance) on (a) the real slab interface and (b) a 3D-uniform random cloud of the same $N$
(\texttt{bench\_fmm.jl} vs \texttt{bench\_fmm\_uniform.jl}). Figure~\ref{fig:throughput} shows the
throughput along each of the three knobs; Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} shows the full
threads$\times N$ throughput maps and their ratio.

\paragraph{The slab is up to $7\times$ slower.} Peak throughput is
$\Theta^{\mathrm{slab}}_{\max}=2.37$ (at 96 threads, $N=93\,312$) versus
$\Theta^{\mathrm{uni}}_{\max}=12.87$ (96 threads, $N=461\,376$). Head-to-head at the largest size
the penalty grows with thread count (Table~\ref{tab:penalty}): from $2.75\times$ single-threaded to
$7.03\times$ at 96 threads ($N=461\,376$).

\paragraph{The penalty factors into ``more work'' $\times$ ``worse scaling''.} At $N=461\,376$,
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{7.03\times}_{\text{96 threads}}
=\underbrace{2.75\times}_{\text{single-threaded}}\;\times\;\underbrace{2.54\times}_{\text{scaling gap}},
\end{equation}
where the single-thread factor is the raw extra work and the scaling gap is the ratio of
parallel speedups: the slab scales $1\!\to\!96$ threads by only $20.4\times$, the uniform cloud by
$52.0\times$ (both at $\nd=36$, $N=461\,376$, from \texttt{fmm\_bench.csv} /
\texttt{fmm\_bench\_uniform.csv}). The cause is the octree: FMM3D's root box is a cube, but the
points occupy only $L_z/L=3.35/90\approx3.7\%$ of its $z$-extent, so the first
$\sim\!\log_2(90/3.35)\approx5$ subdivision levels split empty space (the tree refines like a 2D
quadtree, branching $\sim$4 not 8) and must go deeper to reach the same leaf occupancy; the
resulting boxes are clustered in a thin sheet, which also load-balances poorly across threads.

\paragraph{Caveat.} The uniform $N=93\,312$ point is anomalous: its throughput is non-monotonic in
$N$ (lower than both $N=33\,696$ and $215\,136$ at every thread count), giving a spurious ratio
$<1$ in Table~\ref{tab:penalty}. This is an unlucky seeded random-point tree and should be re-run
with a different seed before being quoted.

\section{Speedup from threads and right-hand-side batching}\label{sec:speedup}

Threads and \nd{} are two orthogonal levers on the FMM kernel
(Fig.~\ref{fig:bench},~\ref{fig:speedup}). We report both relative to the slab kernel, since that
is the production geometry.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fmm_bench.png}
\caption{Per-RHS FMM time (slab). (a) thread scaling at $\nd=36$: near-ideal to $\sim$16 threads,
then saturating. (b) amortization over \nd{} at 16 threads: falls and plateaus.
Data: \texttt{../data/fmm\_bench.csv}.}
\label{fig:bench}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Batching \nd: a flat $\sim$3.7$\times$ that plateaus.} At fixed threads the per-RHS FMM
time $t_{\mathrm{fmm}}/\nd$ falls and saturates (Table~\ref{tab:nd}, $N=461\,376$, 1 thread): the
factor is $3.72\times$ at $\nd=36$ and essentially independent of $N$ and thread count. It cannot
exceed $\sim$4$\times$ here; \S\ref{sec:multirhs} explains why.

\section{How multi-RHS batching accelerates the solve}\label{sec:multirhs}

For one orbital centre $i$, the neighbour pair densities $\rho_{ij}=\varphi_i\varphi_j$ are all
right-hand sides of the \emph{same} operator $A=\tfrac12\gamma^{-1}+D^{\!\top}$. They are therefore
solved together by block GMRES, each matrix--vector product being a single $\nd=K$ batched FMM.
Two layers of acceleration follow.

\subsection{FMM level: why batched densities are cheap}\label{sec:fmmwhy}

A single \texttt{lfmm3d} call is \emph{not} dominated by tree construction. Calling
\texttt{lfmm3d\_ndiv} directly and reading its returned six-phase \texttt{timeinfo}
(\texttt{tools/nd\_timing.f90}; $N=200\,000$ uniform, 1 thread) gives Table~\ref{tab:phases}.

\caption{FMM phase breakdown (s) at $\nd=1$ and $\nd=36$, with the $a+b\,\nd$ split

\label{tab:phases}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}

Tree construction is only $\sim$\SI{0.09}{s}, $\approx$2\% of the call (the ``tree+setup'' row
grows with \nd{} solely because it includes zeroing the \nd-times-larger expansion workspace, not
tree building). The shared $\sim$84\% is \textbf{per-interaction geometry and translation
operators}, dominated by M2L ($1.64$\,s) and the near-field P2P ($1.02$\,s). The FMM3D Fortran
shows directly why this is reused across all densities: every kernel computes the geometry once and
loops over \nd{} for cheap multiply--adds. For the direct (near) kernel \texttt{l3ddirectcg}:

\begin{lstlisting}
cd  = inv4pi/sqrt(dd)            ! 1/r : the expensive sqrt, ONCE per source-target pair
cd1 = -cd/dd
do idim = 1,nd                  ! inner loop over densities: only FMAs
   pot(idim,i)    = pot(idim,i)    + cd *charge(idim,j)
   grad(idim,1,i) = grad(idim,1,i) + cd1*zdiff(1)*charge(idim,j)
   ...
enddo
\end{lstlisting}

and likewise in the far field the associated-Legendre recurrences (\texttt{ylgndrfw}) and the M2L
operators are formed once per box and reused inside a \texttt{do idim=1,nd} loop
(\texttt{l3dmpmp}, \texttt{l3dmpeval}). Hence
$t_{\mathrm{fmm}}(\nd{=}36)\approx 6.8\,t_{\mathrm{fmm}}(\nd{=}1)$ here (not $36\times$), and the
per-RHS ceiling is $(a+b)/b\approx6\times$ for uniform; the slab's heavier near-field and deeper
tree lower the shared fraction, giving the $\sim$3.7--4$\times$ of Table~\ref{tab:nd}.

\subsection{Solve level: block GMRES inherits the FMM's $\sim$4$\times$}

Instrumenting the block solve (\texttt{bench\_solve\_breakdown.jl}, $N=219\,456$, threads pinned)
gives Table~\ref{tab:solve}. Two facts pin down the mechanism:

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering
\caption{Block-solve cost breakdown (s); \texttt{nmv}=operator applications. Data:
\texttt{../data/solve\_breakdown.csv}.}
\label{tab:solve}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrrrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
OMP & $K$ & iter & nmv & total & FMM & matmul & glue & GMRES & per-RHS \\
\midrule
1  & 1  & 7 & 7 & 61.4  & 57.9 (94\%) & 0.015 & 0.70 & 2.83 & 61.4 \\
1  & 36 & 7 & 7 & 552.6 & 537.4 (97\%) & 0.232 & 12.7 & 2.25 & \textbf{15.3} \\
16 & 1  & 7 & 7 & 11.6  & 8.1 (70\%)  & 0.010 & 0.65 & 2.85 & 11.6 \\
16 & 36 & 7 & 7 & 91.6  & 77.6 (85\%) & 0.230 & 12.7 & 1.06 & \textbf{2.54} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The FMM dominates the solve} (94--97\% at 1 thread, 70--85\% at 16); the
near-correction matmul is negligible ($<0.5\%$) and GMRES is a small fixed overhead ($\sim$1--3\,s).
\item \textbf{Block GMRES does not save iterations}: \texttt{iter}\,=\,\texttt{nmv}\,=\,7 for both
$K=1$ and $K=36$. It applies the operator the same number of times regardless of $K$; the sole
benefit is that each application is one $\nd=K$ batched FMM.
\end{enumerate}

Consequently the per-RHS \emph{solve} amortization is $61.4/15.3=4.0\times$ (1 thread) and
$11.6/2.54=4.55\times$ (16 threads)---it simply \emph{tracks the FMM} (whose own $\nd$ factor is
$537.4/57.9=9.3\times$ for 36 densities, i.e.\ $3.9\times$ per RHS). Sweeping $K$
(\texttt{bench\_per\_rhs.jl}, 16 threads) confirms the per-RHS solve time \emph{plateaus} at the
FMM ceiling rather than falling as $1/K$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:perrhs}): from \SI{15.63}{s} ($K=1$) to
\SI{3.62}{s} ($K=36$), a $4.3\times$ amortization.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{per_rhs_runtime.png}
\caption{Per-RHS block-solve time vs.\ $K$ (pinned 16 threads). The data plateau at the FMM

\paragraph{Methodological note.} An earlier unpinned run of \texttt{bench\_per\_rhs.jl} reported a
spurious $\sim$18$\times$ ``per-RHS speedup tracking $1/K$''. With threads unpinned, its small-$K$
solves ran effectively single-threaded while its large-$K$ solves picked up OpenMP/BLAS threads,
folding a $\sim$4$\times$ thread speedup onto the $K$ axis. The arithmetic is decisive: at 1 thread
the $K=36$ FMM alone is \SI{537}{s} (Table~\ref{tab:solve}), so the \SI{127}{s} total that run
reported for $K=36$ is impossible single-threaded. The honest number is $\sim$4$\times$; threads
and \nd{} are separate levers and must be measured with both \texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS} and
\texttt{OPENBLAS\_NUM\_THREADS} pinned.

\section{Summary and recommendations}\label{sec:summary}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Geometry} (\S\ref{sec:geom}): the thin slab interface costs up to $7\times$ a uniform
cloud of equal $N$---$2.75\times$ in raw work (cubic octree wasting the slab's thin dimension) and
$2.5\times$ in worse thread scaling. This, not the kernel or tolerance, is the dominant inefficiency.
\item \textbf{Threads} (\S\ref{sec:speedup}): run at $\sim$32--64 cores; the FMM saturates and 96
threads buys nothing ($\sim$18--20$\times$ over a single thread at large $N$).
\item \textbf{Batching} (\S\ref{sec:speedup},~\ref{sec:multirhs}): always solve co-located densities
as a block---a free $\sim$3.7--4$\times$ per RHS. Combined with threads this is $\sim$73$\times$.
\item \textbf{Mechanism} (\S\ref{sec:multirhs}): the multi-RHS win comes entirely from the batched
FMM, where $\sim$84\% of a call (M2L operators, the $1/r$ near field, Legendre recurrences) is
computed once and reused across all densities; tree construction is $\sim$2\%. Block GMRES adds no
iteration savings.
\item \textbf{Open items}: thread the single-threaded ``glue'' (charge assembly + $\nabla\!\cdot n$
contraction), which reaches $\sim$14\% of the solve at 16 threads and grows with core count;
re-run the anomalous uniform $N=93\,312$ point; an octree matched to the near-2D distribution is
where the remaining geometry factor lives.
\end{itemize}
